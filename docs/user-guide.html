HomeNet Watch User Guide

Keep an eye on every device at home, see what your computer talks to on the internet, and get plain-language alerts when something changes.

HomeNet Watch is made by DDID — DaleDumbsITDown.com. It runs on Windows, macOS and Linux. Everything it records stays on your computer.

  • Your home network: finds every device, checks it's online and how quickly it answers, draws a map, and alerts you when something drops off, slows down or appears for the first time.
  • Your computer's internet use: live and past upload/download, which programs connect where, countries on a world map, and who made each program.
  • Your NAS: storage space, disk health, temperatures, processor and traffic for Synology, QNAP and UGREEN boxes (and routers or computers that share stats).
  • Extras: plain-language insights, a speed test, weekly reports, backup and restore.

Installing

Download the file for your computer from the project's Releases page on GitHub:

Your computerFileHow to start it
Windows 10 / 11homenet-watch-windows-x86_64.exePut it in a folder of your own (for example Documents\HomeNet Watch) and double-click it.
Mac (Intel or Apple silicon)homenet-watch-macos-universalIn Terminal: chmod +x homenet-watch-macos-universal, then right-click the file → Open.
Linux (64-bit PC)homenet-watch-linux-x86_64chmod +x homenet-watch-linux-x86_64, then run it. Needs ping and curl (installed on almost every system).
"Windows protected your PC" or a Mac security message HomeNet Watch isn't code-signed yet, so the first time you open it Windows may show a blue warning: click More info → Run anyway. On a Mac, right-click → Open, or go to System Settings → Privacy & Security and click Open Anyway. You only need to do this once.
Tip: keep it out of Program Files If the program sits in a folder of your own, it can update itself with one click. In protected folders like Program Files it can't replace its own file, so you'd download updates by hand.

First start

  1. HomeNet Watch finds your router and starts looking for devices on your home network. This takes about 20–40 seconds; a progress bar shows at the top right.
  2. Every device it finds gets a name (from the network, or something like "Device .42"), a guessed type (phone, TV, printer, NAS…) and starts being checked every 30 seconds.
  3. An Internet entry checks a public address (1.1.1.1) so you can tell your internet is down even when the router is fine.
  4. Your computer's own traffic and programs start being recorded right away; the history fills in while the app is open.
Phones, tablets, laptops, TVs, speakers and game consoles These come and go, sleep or go into standby, so their "went offline" alerts start switched off. "Slow" alerts are only raised for your router, the internet, NAS boxes and network gear. You can turn alerts on for any device in its details.
It keeps watching when you close the window Closing the window hides HomeNet Watch; it keeps checking and alerting. On Windows and Mac open it again from its icon by the clock (Windows: you may need to click the little arrow to see it). See Running in the background.

Under Your network at the bottom left you always see this computer's address, your router's address, your outside (internet) address, and the current download/upload speed.

The pages

Dashboard

The overview: whether the internet is connected, how many devices are online, which need attention, and which are offline. Click a tile to jump to those devices. Below that are the internet and router response times (30 minutes, 1 hour or 6 hours), a device-status ring, a Needs attention list and the most recent alerts.

Colours used everywhere: online slow or partly answering offline checking paused.

Devices

Every device in one list. Search by name, address or maker; filter by Online, Needs attention, Offline or by room; click a column title to sort. + Add device watches something the scan didn't find, even outside your home (a website or game server).

Click a device to open its details panel on the right:

  • Check now / Pause / Remove. Pausing stops checks and alerts (for example while a device is away for repair).
  • Response: current, average, best and worst response time, plus a strip of recent checks (green answered, amber slow, red missed).
  • Details: rename it, choose its type, set a room / group (like "Office"), and see its address, hardware address, maker, first/last seen, uptime and outages.
  • What to watch:
    • Answers to ping: the normal "are you there?" check.
    • Check it is present: for devices that ignore ping (many phones): it asks the device for its hardware address instead.
    • Alert me…: whether this device may raise alerts.
    • Services (ports): check a specific service, like 80 (web page) or 445 (file sharing). Ports seen during the last scan are offered as one-click buttons.
    • Check a web page loads: fetches a page (plain http) and can require certain words on it.
    • Check it can look up web addresses (DNS): useful for your router or the Internet entry.
    • System stats (SNMP): read storage, disks, temperature, processor and traffic; see Setting up your NAS.
  • Recent events for this device, and your own notes.

Network map

Internet at the top, your router below it, and every device in rows underneath, coloured by status. Offline devices get a dashed red line. Drag devices to arrange them; Arrange automatically puts everything back. With many devices, use Group by Type or Group by Room at the top to sort them into labelled bands (rooms come from each device's Room / group). Click a device for its details.

Traffic

How much this computer downloads and uploads: right now, today, and over the last 7 days compared with the week before.

  • Live: the last 5 minutes, download above the line, upload below.
  • History: choose 1 hour, 6 hours, 24 hours, 7 days or 14 days. Drag the Timeline slider to look back in time; Now jumps back. Hover a bar for exact numbers.

History is recorded while HomeNet Watch is open and kept for 14 days.

Apps & sites

Which programs on this computer use the internet, and where they connect. Four tabs:

  • Programs: each program with its trust status (signed by its maker, part of the operating system, not signed, or trusted by you), how many connections are open now, today and in total, and the data it used.
  • Destinations: every internet place contacted, with its name, category (Advertising, Analytics, Content delivery, Updates, Cloud, Social, Streaming, Gaming), country, programs and ports.
  • Countries: a world map of where traffic goes (turn on country lookup first; see Privacy). Click a country (a dot on the map or its name below) to see which programs connect there, how many connections they make and which places they reach.
  • Connections now: live list of open connections.

Click any program or destination for its details. For a program you'll see where it lives, what started it, who signed it, its fingerprint (SHA-256) and the places it connects to. Trust this version remembers the fingerprint: if the program file ever changes (for example it's tampered with), you get an alert. After a normal update, simply trust the new version.

Data per program On Windows, counting data per program needs administrator rights: click Restart as administrator. Without it, connections are still counted. On Mac and Linux, connection counts are shown.

Save as spreadsheet writes the programs and destinations as two CSV files to your Documents folder.

Insights

What stands out, in plain words:

  • Worth a closer look: programs that changed since you trusted them, aren't signed, run from odd places like Downloads or Temp, talk to many countries, or check in with the same place like clockwork.
  • What changed since yesterday: new programs, new internet places and countries, new devices at home, devices not seen lately, and today's activity compared with yesterday. (On the first day everything is new, so you'll see a summary instead.)
  • Phoning home: programs that went online in many different hours of the day.
  • Ads & tracking: programs contacting advertising or analytics services.

Click an item to open the program, destination or device.

Speed test

Press Start test to measure response time, download and upload using Cloudflare's public speed test. A test takes about 20 seconds and uses roughly 50–200 MB of data. Results are kept under Recent results.

Getting a true reading Pause big downloads and streaming first. For your connection's full speed, test from a computer plugged into the router with a cable.

Performance (NAS)

One card per device that shares its stats: processor, memory and temperature gauges, storage (volumes show free space; storage pools show how much is assigned to volumes, and a fully assigned pool is normal), each disk with model, temperature and health, network traffic and the last hour's trend. Problems such as a failing disk, a degraded storage pool, a full volume or high temperature appear at the top of the card and as alerts.

Details opens the device; ✖ Remove stops reading its stats (it stays in Devices). Add devices with Read stats from a device at the bottom.

Alerts

Everything that happened, newest first. Filter by All, Not seen yet, Critical or Security (alerts linked to a known attacker technique). Click an alert to read what it means, what you can do, and for security alerts its MITRE ATT&CK technique with its name and the attacker's goal (the "tactic"), for example T1557.002 ARP Cache Poisoning (Credential Access, Collection). Click it to read more on attack.mitre.org. From there you can also open the device or Mute alerts like this. Save as spreadsheet exports every alert with its explanation and technique.

When something critical happens (like a device going offline), a red bar appears across the top until you click View alerts or Mark as seen. HomeNet Watch can also play a sound and flash its taskbar/Dock icon (see Settings).

The Tuning tab shows, for each type of alert, its MITRE ATT&CK technique (if any), how many arrived in the last 30 days and how many you actually opened. Types you never open are good candidates to mute; muted alerts are still recorded, just hidden and silent.

Reports

Availability of every device (how often it answered), average and worst response, outages and total time offline.

  • This week's report saves a standalone web page to your Documents folder and opens it: usage compared with last week, busiest programs, what's new, countries, alerts and speed tests. It works offline and can be shared.
  • Save as spreadsheet (CSV) exports the device table.

Settings

At the top is the Checkup: one-click tests for the things that are worth confirming once, each with a plain result underneath:

  • Icon by the clock: whether it's there, and Hide the window now so you can practise bringing it back.
  • Start with the computer: on or off, and if it points at an older or moved copy of HomeNet Watch, Fix it.
  • Phone and email alerts: Send a test buttons.
  • Secure NAS stats (SNMP v3): Test sign-in now for each NAS using v3, or step-by-step Synology instructions if none does yet. The same button is in the NAS's details panel.
  • In-app updates: whether this copy can update itself, and Check the latest release: type your repository (owner/name) to confirm the newest GitHub release has the Windows, Mac and Linux files, the fingerprints and the user guide.

Running in the background

HomeNet Watch only watches while it's running, so it's built to stay running quietly:

  • Closing the window doesn't stop it. The window hides and HomeNet Watch keeps checking your network, recording traffic and sending alerts. (Turn this off in Settings → Running in the background if you'd rather it quit.)
  • Windows and Mac: the icon by the clock. Click the gold HomeNet Watch icon to open the window again. Right-click (Mac: click) it for Open, Pause or resume monitoring and Quit. On Windows the icon may be tucked behind the little ^ arrow; drag it out to keep it visible.
  • Linux: the window is minimised to your taskbar instead.
  • Starting it again while it's already running simply brings the window back; there's only ever one copy.
  • Start with the computer: in Settings → Running in the background, tick Start HomeNet Watch when I sign in. It then starts hidden each time you sign in.
  • Pop-ups: while the window isn't in front, warnings and critical alerts appear as notifications from your system (not during quiet hours).
  • Quitting for real: use Quit on the icon by the clock, or Settings → Quit HomeNet Watch now.

Alerts on your phone or by email

Settings → Phone & email alerts. Choose whether to send critical alerts only (something went offline, a disk failed, router impersonation…) or warnings too, and whether quiet hours hold them back. At most 20 messages an hour are sent, and several alerts at once are combined into one message. Your computer sends them, so HomeNet Watch needs to be running (it can be in the background).

Phone notifications with ntfy (free)

  1. Install the free ntfy app on your phone (Google Play, F-Droid or the App Store).
  2. In HomeNet Watch tick Send alerts to my phone. A hard-to-guess topic name is filled in for you; click Copy.
  3. In the ntfy app tap + (Subscribe to topic), paste the topic name and keep the server as ntfy.sh.
  4. Click Send a test to my phone; a notification should arrive within seconds.
Keep the topic private Anyone who knows the topic name can read your alerts, so treat it like a password. Click New for a fresh one at any time (then subscribe to the new name). If you run your own ntfy server, put its address in Server.

Email

  1. Tick Send alerts by email and click Gmail, iCloud or Yahoo to fill in the mail server, or type your provider's (usually port 587, or 465).
  2. Enter your email address as the user name, and an app password. Gmail, iCloud and Yahoo don't accept your normal password here: create an app password in your account's security settings (Google: Security → 2-Step Verification → App passwords; Apple: account.apple.com → App-Specific Passwords; Yahoo: Account security → Generate app password).
  3. Fill in Send to (several addresses: separate them with commas) and click Send a test email.

The connection to your mail server is always encrypted. On Windows the password is saved encrypted for your Windows account; it is never included in backups.

Setting up your NAS

NAS boxes share their stats through SNMP, which is usually switched off. Turn it on in the NAS first:

NASWhere
Synology (DSM)Control Panel → Terminal & SNMP → SNMP tab → tick Enable SNMP service.
QNAP (QTS)Control Panel → Network & File Services → SNMP → enable the SNMP service.
UGREEN (UGOS)In the Control Panel, find the SNMP setting (under network or services) and switch it on.

Then choose how HomeNet Watch signs in, and enter the same settings in the NAS's details panel in HomeNet Watch (Devices → click the NAS → System stats):

  • v1 / v2c (simple): a "community" word, usually public. Easy, but sent unencrypted on your home network.
  • v3 (secure): a user name with an authentication type (MD5, SHA-1 or SHA-256) and password, and optional privacy (encryption) with DES or AES-128 and a second password. Use AES and SHA where you can.

Within a minute the NAS appears on the Performance page. If it doesn't, the details panel shows why (for example "wrong authentication password or protocol" or "the device doesn't know this SNMP v3 user name").

Your passwords On Windows, SNMP v3 passwords are saved encrypted for your Windows account. On Mac and Linux they're kept in your private settings file, readable only by you. Backups never contain them.

Backup, restore and moving to a new computer

  1. Settings → Backup & restore → Make a backup. Tick Include traffic history if you want that too (bigger file). The file is saved to your Documents folder as HomeNetWatch-backup-…json.
  2. To restore (on this or another computer), put the backup file in Documents, open Settings → Backup & restore and click the file's name.
  3. HomeNet Watch checks the file's fingerprint first: damaged or edited files are refused. It then shows what's inside and changes nothing until you pick what to restore and click Restore selected.
  4. After moving to a new computer, enter any SNMP v3 passwords again (backups never contain passwords).

Updates

Go to About → Check for updates. If a new version is available you'll see what's new and a Download and install button. HomeNet Watch downloads it, checks its fingerprint against the published one (and refuses anything that doesn't match), swaps it in, and offers Restart now. Your devices, settings and history are kept.

It also checks quietly when it starts; you can turn that off in About.

Privacy

Everything HomeNet Watch records stays on your computer. It only contacts the internet for:

  • Your outside address: asks api.ipify.org every 30 minutes.
  • Speed tests: only when you press Start (speed.cloudflare.com).
  • Countries on the world map: only if you turn it on. Internet addresses (never your home devices, never content) are sent to ip-api.com.
  • Checks you set up yourself (web pages, DNS).
  • Updates: asks api.github.com for the newest version (can be turned off).
  • Phone alerts (only if you turn them on): the alert text goes through ntfy.sh, or your own ntfy server.
  • Email alerts (only if you turn them on): sent through your own mail provider.

Troubleshooting

No devices were found

Check Settings → Finding devices: the address range should match your home network (click Use my network). A VPN can hide your home network; pause it and scan again.

A device shows offline but it's on

Many phones, TVs and smart plugs ignore ping or doze. In its details, untick Answers to ping and tick Check it is present, or watch a port it offers.

Lots of "slow" devices

Wi-Fi gadgets that sleep often answer slowly. Raise Call a device slow above in Settings, or mute "Slow responses" in Alerts → Tuning.

My NAS shows "no answer"

Make sure SNMP is switched on in the NAS, the version matches, and the community or v3 user and passwords are exactly the same in both places. Some NAS firewalls block SNMP (UDP port 161); allow it for your home network.

The Data column in Apps & sites is empty

On Windows use Restart as administrator. On Mac and Linux only connection counts are available for now.

Update says the program is in a protected folder

Move HomeNet Watch to a folder of your own (for example in Documents), or download the new version from the release page.

The speed test is slower than my plan

Wi-Fi, other devices and busy evenings all affect results. Test with a cable to the router and nothing else running.

Where did the window go?

Closing it hides it while HomeNet Watch keeps watching. Click its icon by the clock (Windows: look behind the ^ arrow), or simply start HomeNet Watch again.

Phone alerts don't arrive

Check that the topic in the ntfy app is exactly the same as in Settings, press Send a test to my phone, and make sure your phone allows notifications from ntfy. Remember only critical alerts are sent unless you chose warnings too.

The test email fails

"Refused the user name or password" almost always means an app password is needed instead of your normal one. Also check the server and port (587 or 465) match your provider's instructions.

Starting over

Settings → Your data → Forget all devices and history. Make a backup first if you might want anything back.

Glossary

IP addressA device's number on a network, like a house number.
Outside addressThe one address your whole home shares on the internet.
Router / gatewayThe box that connects your home to the internet and hands out addresses.
MAC (hardware) addressA device's built-in network ID. Phones may show a different "private" one per network.
Ping / response timeA tiny "are you there?" message, and how long the answer takes (ms; lower is better).
PortA numbered door for one kind of service, like 80 for web pages.
DNSThe internet's phone book: turns names like example.com into addresses.
Mb/sMegabits per second; 8 Mb/s moves about 1 megabyte each second.
CDNCompanies that keep copies of websites close to you so they load quickly.
Analytics / telemetryData apps send home about how they're used.
Digital signatureA seal showing who made a program and that it hasn't changed since.
Fingerprint (SHA-256)A code calculated from a file; change one byte and it changes.
ARP spoofingA device pretending to be your router to secretly read traffic.
NAS / RAID / storage poolA box of disks on your network; disks working together so one can fail safely; space set aside for volumes.
SNMPA standard way for NAS boxes and routers to share their stats.
MITRE ATT&CKA public catalogue of attacker techniques; some alerts show a matching number for reference.
